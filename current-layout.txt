011                                                 A BSTRACT
012
013            Sparse supervision is a key challenge in multi-stage manipulation, with the lack
014            of supervision over the past-to-future evolution of objects required by the policy
015            being particularly critical. To address this issue, we introduce the Object-State
016
               Transition Map (OST-Map), a unified object-centric representation that organizes
               task-relevant objects as identity-consistent graphs and represents historical and
017
               future object states in a shared structured space. Building on OST-Map, we pro-
018
               pose OSTRA, a diffusion-based Object-State Transition Reasoning Architecture
019            that hierarchically predicts future object states, target TCP poses, and continuous
020            action trajectories. OSTRA combines Transition-Guided Attention, which priori-
021            tizes historical object-state transitions during future-state prediction, with Staged
022            Denoising, which progressively translates predicted object changes into robot sub-
023            goals and executable actions. Experiments on three simulation benchmarks and
024            seven diverse real-world tasks demonstrate consistent improvements over repre-
025            sentative imitation learning baselines. Further ablations validate the contributions
026            of OST-Map and the proposed architectural designs.

029   1    I NTRODUCTION
030
031   Sparse supervision is a key challenge in imitation learning, particularly for multi-stage robot ma-
032   nipulation tasks (Zare et al., 2024; Mandlekar et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2025).
033
      Completing such tasks requires a policy not only to understand the environment at the current time
      step, but also to retain knowledge of the past—for example, whether prior subtasks succeeded and
034
      how key objects changed (Figure 1, left) (Kang & Kuo, 2025; Mark et al., 2026). Because an envi-
035
      ronment consists of objects, the policy must understand how these objects have changed over time.
036   Therefore, the currently observed environment alone is insufficient for the policy to predict the most
037   appropriate action. The policy must also be aware of object changes within the environment. We
038   refer to this challenge as the object-state transition supervision gap.
039
      Early imitation learning methods improve short-term action prediction through action chunking or
040
      limited observation windows, but do not explicitly capture long-horizon task-relevant state transi-
041
      tions (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2025). Recent approaches incorporate past information through
042   observation histories, latent memories, or task progress (Liang & Boularias, 2021; Shi et al., 2026;
043   Mark et al., 2026). Others use future observations, goal states, or latent plans as additional supervi-
044   sion (Lynch et al., 2020; Mandlekar et al., 2020; Belkhale & Sadigh, 2023). However, these methods
045   represent temporal information at observation, latent, or task level, providing limited supervision on
046   individual-object evolution during execution. Our method bridges this gap by unifying both tempo-
047   ral directions at the object level. Specifically, it represents past object-state transitions as structured
048   historical context and predicts future object states as auxiliary supervision, enabling the policy to
049   reason about how task-relevant objects have changed and how they should evolve next.
050   To provide such supervision over past-to-future object evolution, we introduce the Object-State
051   Transition Map (OST-Map), an object-centric representation of observed and future states in a
052   shared structured space. OST-Map avoids requiring policies to discover transition-relevant cues
053   from visual histories using robot-centric action supervision alone. Specifically, OST-Map represents
      each task-relevant object as an identity-consistent graph of structural nodes. Each node encodes

      Building on OST-Map, we introduce OSTRA, an Object-State Transition Reasoning Architecture
      for multi-stage manipulation (Figure 1, middle). OSTRA is a diffusion-based framework that hierar-
079
      chically predicts future object states, target TCP poses, and continuous action trajectories. It incor-
080
      porates two key designs. First, Transition-Guided Attention attends to historical OST-Map tokens
081   before incorporating dense Semantic Field tokens, encouraging the model to prioritize task-relevant
082   object-state transitions when predicting future states. Second, Staged Denoising follows an object-
083   to-TCP-to-action hierarchy that progressively translates predicted object-state changes into robot
084   subgoals and executable actions. Together, these designs connect object-level transition reasoning
085   with robot-level planning and continuous control, enabling the policy to reason from how objects
086   have changed to how they should evolve next and which actions should realize those changes.
087   We evaluate OSTRA on three simulation benchmarks and seven diverse real-world tasks. Across
088   these settings, OSTRA consistently improves over representative imitation learning baselines on
089   multi-stage manipulation tasks. Further ablation studies validate the contributions of OST-Map,
090   Transition-Guided Attention, and Staged Denoising, demonstrating the effectiveness of structured
091   object-state transition supervision for connecting scene evolution with robot action generation.
092
      Our contributions are: (1) To tackle the object-state transition supervision gap in multi-stage imita-
093   tion learning, we introduce OST-Map, an object-centric representation that aligns historical object-
094   state sequences with future object-state targets, enabling direct supervision of object-state transi-
095   tions. (2) Building on OST-Map, we introduce OSTRA, a diffusion-based architecture with two key
096   designs. a) Transition-Guided Attention applies cross-attention to OST-Map tokens before Semantic
097   Field tokens, encouraging the model to prioritize object-state transition information. b) Staged De-
098   noising hierarchically predicts future object states, TCP poses, and action trajectories, progressively
099   translating object-level state changes into robot subgoals and continuous control. (3) We conduct
100   evaluations across three simulation benchmarks and seven diverse real-world tasks, demonstrating
101
      consistent performance gains over representative imitation learning baselines.

106
107   Long-horizon manipulation requires policies to maintain coherent behavior across extended execu-
      tion. Existing methods reduce the effective control horizon through action chunking, autoregressive

      prediction, or diffusion-based action generation (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2025; Haldar et al.,
109   2024; Reuss et al., 2024). Other approaches introduce higher-level task structure through subgoals,
110   skill composition, progress estimation, or planning (Shiarlis et al., 2018; Gupta et al., 2020; Chen
111   et al., 2023; Kang & Kuo, 2025; Jain et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Feng et al.,
112   2025; Zhou et al., 2026). These methods improve extended execution by organizing actions or task
113   stages over time, but generally do not explicitly supervise how task-relevant objects evolve from
114   historical states toward future target states.
115
116   2.2   T EMPORAL M ODELING IN ROBOT P OLICIES
117
118   Temporal context is commonly incorporated through historical observations, latent memory states,
119   recurrent features, or selectively retrieved past frames (Liang & Boularias, 2021; Fang et al., 2025;
120
      Shi et al., 2026; Mark et al., 2026). Recent memory-augmented policies further maintain recurrent
      or hierarchical memory to capture dependencies beyond the current observation (Ji et al., 2026; Xiao
121
      et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026).
122
123   A complementary direction exploits future information as additional guidance. Existing methods
124   condition policies on goal observations or latent plans (Lynch et al., 2020; Mandlekar et al., 2020),
125   predict future visual states or joint video-action trajectories (Hu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), or
126
      learn predictive representations from future transitions and interaction targets (Zeng et al., 2024).
      Despite incorporating complementary historical and future context, these approaches primarily rep-
127
      resent temporal information through holistic observations, latent memories, predicted frames, or
128
      task-level targets, rather than explicitly modeling identity-consistent state transitions of individual
129   objects over time.
130
131
      2.3   O BJECT-C ENTRIC R EPRESENTATIONS FOR M ANIPULATION
132
133   Object-centric policies organize perception and action around manipulable entities rather than holis-
134   tic scene features. Existing approaches use object proposals or 3D object features as policy inputs
135   (Zhu et al., 2023b;a), learn affordance-, flow-, or contact-based interaction representations (Belkhale
136   & Sadigh, 2023; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Lee & Fazeli, 2025), or represent manipula-
137   tion through explicit object motion, including SE(3) pose trajectories and object pointflow (Hsu
138   et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Recent work further introduces structured object-level intermediate
139   representations based on physical concepts or semantic operations (Wei et al., 2026; Tang et al.,
140
      2026).
141   Object-centric world models further use entity-level representations for dynamics and future pre-
142   diction (Ferraro et al., 2025; Jeong et al., 2025), while graph-based approaches preserve explicit
143   objects and relations for manipulation planning (Zhu et al., 2026). These methods typically repre-
144   sent objects as perceptual units, interaction interfaces, motion variables, or future prediction targets.
145   In contrast, OST-Map maintains identity-consistent structural states across both observation history
146
      and future task stages, enabling past-to-future object-state transitions to serve as explicit intermedi-
      ate supervision for action generation.

354   We further evaluate OSTRA on a 7-DoF Realman 75-BF robot arm equipped with a Pika parallel
355   gripper. RGB-D observations are captured by an Intel RealSense D415 camera, and point clouds are
356   reconstructed using Open3D (Zhou et al., 2018). The map constructor operates on predicted object
357   masks, poses, and structural parameters rather than simulator state. This setting therefore evaluates
358   both the robustness of object-state reasoning and the effect of errors accumulated by the OST-Map
359   perception pipeline.
360
361   Task suite. We consider seven tasks covering articulated interaction, long-horizon rearrangement,
362   thin or reflective objects, and global spatial constraints. Place Fork in Drawer requires opening
363   a drawer, grasping a fork, placing it inside, and closing the drawer. Stack Three Blocks requires
364   two sequential placements while preserving the state of the partial stack. Arrange Food with Fork
365
      tests compositional placement of multiple objects in a cluttered scene. Place Bread in Microwave
      combines door manipulation, object transfer, partial occlusion, and door closure. Build Letter M
366
      with Blocks requires arranging five blocks into a specified global pattern and is the longest task in
367
      the suite. Collect Blocks requires gathering multiple displaced blocks into a target container, while
368   Cook the Pear requires manipulating two food items through a shared cooking setup. These tasks,
369   visualized in Figure 4, expose different forms of object-state transition: articulated state, support
370   relations, containment, occupancy, and multi-object layout.

376   reliable multi-stage behavior on the drawer and microwave tasks and better preserves inter-object

      Letter M with Blocks (65% versus 15%), supporting our claim that explicit object-state transition

405   4.3     S YSTEM E RROR B REAKDOWN
406
407   We use combinatorial ground-truth replacements to identify which errors in the OST-Map Construc-
408   tor most affect downstream task success.
409
410   Setup. We examine three components: object extraction by SAM 3, object-pose estimation and
411   tracking by FoundationPose, and structural-size estimation by the PointNet–MLP estimator. Their
412   outputs are replaced with ground-truth masks, poses, or size parameters, respectively, while the
413
      policy weights and evaluation conditions remain fixed. The constructor processes these inputs to
      form the maps supplied to the policy. We evaluate all eight combinations, including Full OSTRA
414
      with no replacements, three single-component replacements, three pairwise replacements, and the
415
      all-GT constructor. Ground-truth quantities are used only for this diagnosis, not during normal
416   deployment.
417
418
      Analysis. Table 3 reports average full-task success and its change in percentage points relative to
419   Full OSTRA. Single-component replacements test the effect of correcting each error source; joint
420   replacements reveal whether their effects interact. The Semantic Field encoder and action policy
421   remain unchanged even in the all-GT setting, so residual failures cannot be attributed solely to map
422   construction.

452   4.5     A BLATION S TUDIES
453
454   We ablate OSTRA along four dimensions: the temporal roles and attributes of OST-Map, the order
455   of map–visual attention, the staged denoising and stop-gradient design, and key temporal and loss
456   hyperparameters. All variants are retrained on RLBench2 with the same data, backbone capacity,
457
      training budget, and evaluation seeds, and are evaluated by mean task success.
458
459   OST-Map. Table 5 compares no map or future-node loss (Ex1), history without future supervision
460
      (Ex2), and future supervision with only the latest map (Ex3), against the full model (Ex11). Ex2,
      Ex3, and Ex11 retain all map attributes. With both temporal roles enabled, Ex4–Ex11 progressively
461
      add geometry, semantics, affordances, and the four edge attribute groups to pose-only nodes. All
462
      map-enabled variants share the graph encoder, constructor outputs, and node correspondence; omit-
463   ted attributes are masked, and edge-attention biases are disabled when no edge attributes remain.
464   The full OST-Map reaches 89.8%, improving over the no-map variant by 20.4 percentage points.
465   History-only and future-only supervision achieve 84.5% and 85.8%, respectively, while their com-
466   bination performs best; the consistent gains from Ex4 to Ex11 further show that both node and edge
467   attributes contribute to performance.
468
469   Transition-Guided Attention. Ex1–Ex4 in Table 6 test map-only, visual-only, joint attention over
470   concatenated tokens, and visual-first attention against map-first attention (Ex12). All retain the
471   complete prediction hierarchy and both stop-gradient boundaries. M → V and V → M denote
472   the two sequential attention orders. Map-first attention achieves 89.8%, outperforming map-only,
473
      visual-only, joint attention, and visual-first attention by 13.3, 11.6, 4.4, and 2.7 percentage points,
      respectively.
474
475
      Staged Denoising. With map-first attention fixed, Ex5–Ex8 test action-only prediction, object-to-
476
      action conditioning, TCP-to-action conditioning, and flat joint prediction. Removed stages lose their
477
      heads and auxiliary losses; the flat variant retains all targets without hierarchical conditioning (Hier.).
478   Ex9–Ex12 test all four combinations of detaching object and TCP features passed downstream, with
479   Ex12 as the full model. In Ex6, object features pass directly to the action stage. Node and TCP errors
480   are recorded where their prediction stages are present. The full hierarchy improves over action-only
481   prediction by 14.0 percentage points and over flat joint prediction by 8.3 points. Applying both
482   stop-gradient boundaries yields a further 3.9-point gain over using neither, confirming the benefit of
483   isolating the three prediction stages.
